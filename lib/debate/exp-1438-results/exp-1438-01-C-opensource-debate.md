## Topic

Considering the inherent tensions between rapid innovation, robust safety, and equitable access, under what conditions can open-source AI development, through the coordinated efforts of developers, users, and policymakers, reliably contribute to long-term societal well-being?

## Opening Statements

### Skeptic (skeptic)

The promise of open-source general-purpose AI development, managed through 'coordinated multi-stakeholder efforts,' is a well-packaged fantasy designed to obscure its true material costs and concentrate power. Some argue that open-source AI inherently democratizes access, empowering countless smaller innovators to build and compete, thereby fostering widespread economic prosperity and resilience. But let's talk about what this machine actually is: a massive, capital-intensive infrastructure project. Who owns the compute? Who controls the semiconductor supply chains? It's a handful of corporations, not a decentralized collective. These are the same corporations whose data centers in Iowa suck down millions of gallons of water and entire towns' worth of electricity, while the environmental footprint remains largely unaccounted for in any public balance sheet. This isn't liberation; it's a funnel for capital, and open-source often just means you're doing the unpaid debugging for the few who capture the profits. Now ask who pays for the water.

The notion of 'coordinated multi-stakeholder efforts' is a polite fiction unless it's backed by genuine democratic control and legally binding civil rights protections. Proponents of multi-stakeholder initiatives contend these forums are vital for building consensus and establishing ethical norms, gradually guiding AI development toward safer, more responsible outcomes. We’ve seen this script before in other tech sectors: these 'multi-stakeholder initiatives' often become industry lobbying fronts, not real accountability mechanisms. Who sits at these tables? Is it the 23-year-old in Nairobi labeling violent content for $2 an hour so your model passes its safety audit, or the communities targeted by biased algorithms? Or is it just the same tech giants and their hand-picked 'experts'? Without the teeth of enforceable legislation, these 'coordinated efforts' are a convenient way for incumbents to write their own rules and pull up the regulatory drawbridge, avoiding any real accountability for the harms they unleash.

While some are busy debating whether AI will become sentient or destroy us all in 50 years, people are losing jobs *today* because of automation, and algorithms are discriminating in housing and credit *today*. Others prioritize either AI's boundless future potential or its catastrophic long-term risks, viewing present-day harms as solvable technical challenges or necessary growing pains. Open-source doesn't magically fix these existing, documented harms; it often just makes the tools for these harms more widely available, faster. Reports from groups like the Algorithmic Justice League have consistently documented how AI systems perpetuate systemic bias in areas from hiring to criminal justice, eroding trust and deepening social inequalities. Unless we fundamentally restructure our economic and social systems to address these existing inequalities and enforce legally binding civil rights constraints on AI development, 'societal well-being' is just a nice phrase for a few, not a reality for the many. Who's going to pay for that restructuring, because right now, nobody is?
